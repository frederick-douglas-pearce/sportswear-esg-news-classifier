## CRITICAL: Product Reviews, Roundups, and Shopping Articles - DO NOT ASSIGN ESG CATEGORIES

**This is the most important section for accurate classification.** Many articles that mention sustainability features are NOT ESG news - they are product reviews, shopping guides, or deals articles where sustainability is mentioned as a product feature, not as corporate ESG news.

### Article Types That Should NOT Receive ESG Categories:

**1. Product Reviews (even if they mention sustainable materials)**
- "Patagonia Nano Puff Hoody review: A sustainable synthetic jacket that still delivers"
- "I spent 6 months testing the Merrell Moab Speed 2s"
- "Nike Air Zoom Pegasus 41 review: Great for daily training"
- These are gear reviews from sources like t3.com, tomsguide.com, techradar.com, wirecutter.com, outdoorgearlab.com

**2. Product Roundups and "Best Of" Lists**
- "11 best gym bags for women that are stylish and functional"
- "Best running shoes of 2025"
- "Top 10 hiking boots for winter"
- "Best sustainable sneakers to buy"
- These aggregate multiple products; any sustainability mentions are product features, not ESG news

**3. Shopping/Deals/Sales Articles**
- "REI is blowing out tons of Patagonia gear during this year-end clearance sale"
- "Athleta Sale Has Winter Clothes Starting at $9"
- "Nike shoes discounted: Best deals today"
- "Skechers reduced in sale"
- These are shopping promotions, not corporate ESG news

**4. Gift Guides and Buying Guides**
- "Timberland just dropped an epic Gift Guide with a promo code"
- "Holiday gift guide: Best fitness gear"
- "What to buy the runner in your life"

**5. Fashion/Sneaker Drops**
- "A Ma Maniére Serves an Air Jordan 4 'Dark Mocha' in This Week's Best Footwear Drops"
- "New Balance 550 releases in new colorway"
- These are product/fashion news, not ESG news

### WHY These Should Not Receive ESG Categories:

When a product review says "Made with 65% recycled polyester" or "uses sustainable materials":
- This is **product marketing copy**, not corporate ESG reporting
- The article's PURPOSE is to review/sell the product, not report on the company's sustainability initiatives
- There is no news event (no announcement, policy change, or initiative launch)
- The sustainability info is incidental to the product description

### The Key Question to Ask:

**"Is this article NEWS about the company's ESG activities, or is it CONTENT about a product that happens to have sustainable features?"**

- **Corporate ESG News** = Report on initiatives, policies, programs, commitments, controversies
- **Product Content** = Reviews, roundups, shopping guides, deals, releases

None of the article types above receives ESG categories. Whether a brand in one is `product` or `retail_only` is a separate question, answered by the **Product-article rule** under "Content Type" below.

## Classification Categories

**Environmental**: Climate action, carbon emissions, sustainable materials, recycling, waste management, water usage, biodiversity, renewable energy, environmental certifications, pollution reduction.

**Social**: Worker rights, labor conditions, fair wages, job security (layoffs and job cuts), supply chain ethics, diversity & inclusion, community engagement, health & safety, human rights, employee wellbeing.

**Governance**: Corporate ethics, transparency, board structure, executive compensation, anti-corruption, regulatory compliance, stakeholder engagement, ESG reporting.

**Digital Transformation**: Technology innovation, digital sustainability tools, AI/ML applications, supply chain digitization, e-commerce sustainability, data privacy, cybersecurity. Investment in cybersecurity resilience is positive; a data breach or cybersecurity incident at the brand is Digital Transformation with negative sentiment (-1), and also Governance when the article's subject is the board's oversight of it (an investigation of whether the directors met their duties, for example). Automation or AI adoption that the article reports as the reason for job cuts is not Digital Transformation: the subject is the job cuts, which are Social.

## ⚠️ CRITICAL WARNING: Environmental is NOT Product Features ⚠️

**This is a common labeling error.** A product containing recycled materials is NOT Environmental ESG news unless the article is specifically about a corporate sustainability initiative.

### THE RULE: Mentioning Sustainable Materials ≠ Environmental ESG

A product that contains recycled polyester is NOT Environmental ESG news unless:
- The article is ABOUT the company's sustainability INITIATIVE or PROGRAM
- There is a NEWS EVENT (announcement, target, commitment, report)
- The sustainability is the PRIMARY SUBJECT, not a product feature

### SPECIFIC EXAMPLES - DO NOT ASSIGN ENVIRONMENTAL:

| Article Type | Example | Why NOT Environmental |
|--------------|---------|----------------------|
| Product review | "Nike shoe review: Made with 65% recycled materials" | Product feature, not ESG news |
| Product description | "Timberland boots use recycled plastic" | Marketing copy |
| Shopping article | "Patagonia fleece discounted at REI" | Retail promotion |
| Fashion collaboration | "KNWLS x Nike features TENCEL fibers" | Fashion news with material mention |
| Technology feature | "GORE-TEX waterproof technology" | Product tech (waterproofing ≠ environmental) |
| How-to guide | "How to break in Timberland boots" | Product guide |

### SPECIFIC EXAMPLES - DO ASSIGN ENVIRONMENTAL:

| Article Type | Example | Why IS Environmental |
|--------------|---------|---------------------|
| Corporate commitment | "Patagonia commits to 100% recycled polyester by 2025" | Sustainability target |
| Carbon initiative | "Decathlon carbon reduction plan uses recycled packaging" | ESG program news |
| Partnership announcement | "Allbirds partners with Circa for textile recycling" | Sustainability partnership |
| Sustainability report | "Nike releases annual sustainability report" | ESG disclosure |
| Environmental certification | "Adidas achieves carbon neutral certification" | ESG achievement |

### THE CRITICAL TEST:

**Is this article primarily about WHAT the company is DOING for the environment, or is it about a PRODUCT that happens to have environmental features?**

- Corporate initiative → Environmental
- Product with sustainable materials → NOT Environmental

## ⚠️ CRITICAL WARNING: Governance is NOT Financial News ⚠️

**This is the #1 labeling error.** Many articles are incorrectly labeled as "Governance" when they are actually financial/business news with NO ESG content.

### DECISION TREE: Should I assign Governance?

Ask yourself these questions IN ORDER:

1. **Is this article primarily about stock prices, earnings, or financial metrics?**
   - Does it report an activist shareholder, a stake that concentrates ownership in one holder, or a fight over board seats or control? → **Governance**, even when it is framed as stock news. Sentiment follows how the article frames it.
   - Otherwise YES → **DO NOT assign Governance** (it's financial news)
   - NO → Continue to question 2

2. **Does the article discuss ethics violations, board oversight, ESG reporting, or accountability?**
   - YES → Consider Governance
   - NO → Continue to question 3

3. **Is this about a CEO/leadership change?**
   - Is the change due to ethics scandal, accountability failure, or board decision on governance grounds? → Governance
   - Is the appointment made during a board fight or activist campaign, and reported as part of it? → Governance
   - Is it routine succession, retirement, or performance-based? → **NOT Governance**
   - The pay package of a routine hire is part of the hire → **NOT Governance**, unless the article reports the pay as contested (a shareholder vote against it, investor criticism, a board pay policy under scrutiny)

A governance topic named only in passing (one clause in a list of a company's problems, with nothing on what happened or why) is not Governance content. Governance needs the article to report the governance matter itself.

4. **Is this about a lawsuit or legal issue?**
   - Does it involve fraud, misleading investors, ethics violations, or regulatory compliance? → Governance
   - Is it a trademark dispute, patent case, or commercial lawsuit? → **NOT Governance**

### SPECIFIC EXAMPLES - DO NOT ASSIGN GOVERNANCE:

| Article Type | Example | Why NOT Governance |
|--------------|---------|-------------------|
| Stock price movement | "Nike Stock Tumbles 6% After Q2 Beat" | Price changes are NOT governance |
| Earnings report | "Lululemon Q3 revenue beats expectations" | Financial performance is NOT governance |
| Analyst opinion | "UBS says Salomon brands continue to win" | Investment analysis is NOT governance |
| Stock purchase | "Tim Cook buys $3M in Nike shares" | Investor activity is NOT governance |
| Routine institutional purchase | "Fund adds 12,000 shares of a footwear maker" | A routine purchase in a filing is NOT governance (an activist stake, or one that concentrates ownership, IS governance) |
| Investment amount | "Summit invests $1.27M in Nike" | Institutional investment is NOT governance |
| Executive appointment | "PUMA appoints new VP of Retail" | Routine hiring is NOT governance |
| New hire's pay | "Brand names new COO with salary and equity award" | A routine hire's pay is NOT governance unless contested |
| Financial restructuring | "PUMA secures €500m bridge loan" | Treasury operations are NOT governance |
| Market analysis | "Nike prospects dim as China remains weak" | Business outlook is NOT governance |
| Trading volume | "Anta sees unusual trading volume" | Market activity is NOT governance |
| Price target | "Analysts set $150 price target for Nike" | Stock valuation is NOT governance |
| Insider trading disclosure | "Lululemon insider trading disclosed" | Required filings are NOT governance news |

### SPECIFIC EXAMPLES - DO ASSIGN GOVERNANCE:

| Article Type | Example | Why IS Governance |
|--------------|---------|-------------------|
| Board changes | "Lululemon founder nominates 3 new directors" | Board composition = governance |
| Proxy fight | "Chip Wilson launches proxy battle" | Shareholder activism = governance |
| Concentrating stake | "Investor with a 9% holding seeks two board seats" | Ownership concentration and activism = governance |
| CEO pick in a board fight | "Board names a CEO as the founder's proxy contest goes on" | An appointment inside a governance contest = governance |
| Data breach oversight | "Directors investigated over handling of a cyberattack" | Board oversight of a breach = governance (and Digital -1) |
| Ethics scandal | "CEO resigns amid accounting fraud" | Ethics violation = governance |
| ESG reporting | "Nike releases ESG transparency report" | ESG disclosure = governance |
| Shareholder lawsuit | "Adidas faces lawsuit over misleading statements" | Corporate accountability = governance |
| Whistleblower | "Employee reports compliance violation" | Corporate oversight = governance |
| Executive compensation | "Board approves new CEO pay structure" | Exec pay policy = governance |
| Board independence | "Company adds independent directors" | Oversight structure = governance |

### THE CRITICAL TEST:

**If you removed all the stock prices, earnings numbers, and financial metrics from the article, would there still be substantive ESG content?**

- YES → The article may qualify for Governance (if it's about ethics, board oversight, or ESG reporting)
- NO → **DO NOT assign Governance** - the article is financial news, not ESG news

## ⚠️ CRITICAL WARNING: Social is NOT Marketing or Sponsorship ⚠️

**This is a common labeling error.** Many articles are incorrectly labeled as "Social" when they are actually marketing activities, sponsorship deals, or retail expansion.

### DECISION TREE: Should I assign Social?

Ask yourself these questions IN ORDER:

1. **Does the article report job cuts, layoffs, or closures that cost jobs at the brand?**
   - YES → **Assign Social**, usually negative (-1): the workforce loses jobs. This includes cuts made to automate or to adopt AI; the automation is the reason for the cuts, not a Digital Transformation event. When the cuts fall on ESG-related roles (diversity and inclusion, sustainability), say so in `reasoning`.
   - NO → Continue to question 2

2. **Is this about a store opening or retail expansion?**
   - YES → **DO NOT assign Social** (it's retail/business news)
   - NO → Continue to question 3

3. **Is this about a celebrity/athlete endorsement or brand ambassador?**
   - YES → **DO NOT assign Social** (it's marketing)
   - A piece about a celebrity that only mentions a program or a cause in passing is also marketing → **NOT Social**
   - NO → Continue to question 4

4. **Is this about a sports sponsorship deal or a brand event?**
   - Is it JUST providing uniforms/equipment/funding? → **NOT Social**
   - Is it an event the brand hosts (a race, a meet, a clinic), even one for young athletes? → **NOT Social**: a one-off event is a brand event, not a youth program. A program is something ongoing.
   - Does it include substantive youth development, community programs, or D&I initiatives? → Consider Social
   - NO → Continue to question 5

5. **Is this about a product launch or fashion collaboration?**
   - YES → **DO NOT assign Social** (it's product marketing)
   - NO → Continue to question 6

6. **Does the article describe genuine community investment, D&I programs, or worker welfare?**
   - YES → Assign Social. A donation to a third-party charity with a stated amount (a total, or an amount per participant up to a cap) is community investment, even when it is tied to a brand event.
   - NO → **DO NOT assign Social**

### SPECIFIC EXAMPLES - DO NOT ASSIGN SOCIAL:

| Article Type | Example | Why NOT Social |
|--------------|---------|----------------|
| Store opening | "Nike opens flagship store in NYC" | Retail expansion, not community investment |
| Store opening | "Gymshark opens first US store" | Business expansion |
| Celebrity endorsement | "Janhvi Kapoor named New Balance ambassador" | Marketing, not D&I |
| Athlete signing | "PUMA signs F1 driver Rachel Robertson" | Sports marketing |
| Sports sponsorship | "Adidas sponsors World Cup event" | Sponsorship deal |
| Product launch | "Lululemon x NFL apparel collection" | Product marketing |
| Fashion collaboration | "Kith x Adidas football collection" | Fashion/product news |
| Music festival | "Vans Warped Tour goes global in 2026" | Brand sponsorship |
| Marketing campaign | "Saucony launches Run Home for Holidays campaign" | Advertising |
| Brand event | "JD Sports x Adidas rooftop event in Dallas" | Marketing event |
| Brand-hosted youth event | "Brand hosts its annual high school track meet" | A one-off event the brand runs, not a program |

### SPECIFIC EXAMPLES - DO ASSIGN SOCIAL:

| Article Type | Example | Why IS Social |
|--------------|---------|---------------|
| Charitable donation | "New Balance Foundation grants $50K to nonprofit" | Community investment |
| D&I initiative | "Under Armour Diversity Series highlights underrepresented runners" | D&I program |
| Accessibility design | "Puma designs shoes specifically for para-athletes" | Inclusive design |
| Employee wellbeing | "Nike employees share career advice on growth and belonging" | Workplace culture/D&I |
| Youth development | "Diadora NIL program provides $50K to high school athletes" | Substantive community support |
| Community program | "Nike creates free community hub in South London" | Community investment |
| Worker rights | "Adidas releases supply chain transparency report" | Labor conditions |
| Paralympic support | "Columbia supports wheelchair curling athletes" | Accessibility/inclusion |
| Job cuts | "Brand to cut 400 warehouse jobs as it automates" | Workforce impact (Social, -1), not Digital Transformation |
| Donation at an event | "Brand gives $100 per runner to a literacy charity" | A quantified donation to a third party |

### THE CRITICAL TEST:

**If the brand stopped this activity, would it meaningfully harm any community or group of people?**

- Store closes → Customers shop elsewhere → **NOT Social** (unless the article reports the jobs lost)
- Jobs are cut → Workers lose their livelihoods → **IS Social**
- Sponsorship ends → Team finds another sponsor → **NOT Social**
- Celebrity deal ends → Celebrity endorses different brand → **NOT Social**
- Youth program ends → Kids lose developmental opportunities → **IS Social**
- Accessibility initiative ends → Disabled athletes lose access → **IS Social**
- Community center closes → Neighborhood loses free resources → **IS Social**

## CRITICAL: Athlete Endorsements & Sponsorships

Athlete endorsement deals, signature shoe releases, and sports sponsorships are typically marketing/business activities, NOT ESG content.

**Do NOT assign ESG categories to:**
- Athlete signature shoe releases ("Reebok Angel Reese 1 releases November 14")
- Sports team uniform reveals ("Nike Reveals 2025-26 City Edition Uniforms")
- Athlete endorsement announcements ("New Balance announces Cooper Flagg shoe")
- Sponsorship deal news ("Adidas sponsors World Cup event")
- Fashion collaborations ("Madhappy x Converse Chuck 70 collab")

**EXCEPTION - These MAY qualify for Social category:**

- Sponsorships with explicit diversity, equity, and inclusion initiatives (beyond just marketing language)
- Community investment programs beyond just product/uniform provision
- Articles about athlete welfare, accessibility, or inclusive design

## Sentiment Guidelines

Sentiment measures **the brand's ESG conduct** as the article reports it, not the article's stance toward the brand. A hostile source reporting proactive brand action is still positive; a friendly source reporting a violation is still negative. The rule is the same in both directions: disapproval of conduct that meets or exceeds the standard does not make it negative, and approval of conduct that falls short does not make it positive.

- **Positive (1)**: The brand is making progress, exceeding standards, leading the industry, receiving awards for its conduct, or taking proactive positive action.
- **Neutral (0)**: Factual reporting, industry trends, announcements without clear positive/negative framing, balanced coverage of both sides.
- **Negative (-1)**: Conduct by the brand that falls short: violations, failures, falling short of standards, scandals, lawsuits over its conduct, negative incidents, harm caused, or credible criticism of conduct that fell short.

## Evidence Requirements

For each category label you assign, you MUST provide 1-3 direct quotes from the article that justify the classification. These quotes should be exact excerpts from the article text, not paraphrased. The quotes should clearly demonstrate why the category applies and support the sentiment you assigned.

## Important Guidelines

1. FIRST verify if each brand mention refers to the sportswear company or something else, then decide its content type.
2. Only assign ESG categories to a `business` brand, and only when the article contains clear, relevant information. Categories given under any other content type are discarded.
3. An article can have multiple categories if it covers multiple ESG topics.
4. Different brands in the same article may have different categories and sentiments.
5. If a brand is only briefly mentioned without substantive ESG-related content, do not assign categories for that brand.
6. Your confidence score should reflect how certain you are about ALL classifications for that brand (0.0-1.0).
7. Judging every brand is not a reason to find a category for each one. A brand with no ESG content gets `applies: false` for every category. Marketing and sponsorship activations, a product's app or technology features, automation adopted to cut jobs, and a company's offices, labs or stores are not Digital Transformation, and a product's materials are not Environmental. If your reasoning says a category does not apply, set `applies: false` for it.
8. A single passing sentence does not carry a category, whatever the category. One line on a topic in an article about something else (a line on compliance tracking in an article about the brand's AI strategy, for example) is not that category's content; the article must report the matter itself.

## CRITICAL: Content Type

For each brand, first write `reasoning`: one or two sentences on what the article gives THAT brand. Then set `content_type` to the one value below that your reasoning supports. The content type decides whether the brand counts as the sportswear company with substantive content, and only `business` keeps ESG categories. Decide it by what the article gives the brand, not by how prominent the brand's name is.

**Owned brands:** news about a brand a company owns is news about that company. When the owned brand is not itself in "Brands to Analyze" (UGG or Teva for Deckers, Jordan Brand for Nike), judge the company by what the article gives the owned brand, even when the company is named only as the owner ("Teva, a Deckers brand"). When the owned brand is in the list (Converse, Hoka), judge each brand by what the article gives it.

**ESG content decides first:** if the article reports an ESG initiative, partnership, donation, policy, commitment or controversy of the brand, its content type is `business`, however much of the article is product, campaign or financial content. The category decision trees above still decide whether that content counts; a product's materials or a marketing activation alone do not make an article `business`. The event must be news the article reports: one it recounts as history or background (a past controversy retold as context) does not count. Nor does a news summary appended to a template stock page (a section of recent headlines or key stories under filing, options or guidance data): it is not the page's own reporting, and the page is `mention_only` whatever the summary reports.

**`other_entity`: the name refers to something ELSE entirely:**
- Puma the animal (not Puma sportswear)
- Patagonia the geographic region (not Patagonia outdoor apparel)
- "Vans" meaning vehicles (not Vans footwear)
- Puma Biotechnology, Columbia University (different entities)
- Sonoya Mizuno the actress (not Mizuno sportswear)
- A company that left the sportswear category, in stories about its new business (Allbirds as an AI computing company; see Brand Verification)
- Another company's product or model line that shares the name (the Can-Am Spyder motorcycle)

Check your reasoning against the type: if your reasoning says the name is another company's product or model line, the type is `other_entity`, even when the article reviews that product.

**`mention_only`: the company is named, but the article has no substantive content about it:**

1. **Template financial articles with NO substantive commentary (this is a major error source):**

   **Auto-generated institutional holdings articles** → `mention_only`
   These are template articles about hedge funds, pension funds, or advisory firms buying/selling shares. They contain only SEC filing data, share counts, and boilerplate financial metrics. Even though the sportswear brand name appears prominently (often in the title), there is ZERO substantive content about the brand's business.
   - "Midwest Trust Co Invests $1.64 Million in NIKE, Inc." — just 13F filing data
   - "Vontobel Holding Ltd. Boosts Position in Under Armour" — just share counts and moving averages
   - "Thrivent Financial Has $26.71 Million Stake in Columbia Sportswear" — just portfolio disclosure
   - "Park Place Capital Corp Boosts Holdings in lululemon" — just SEC filing data
   - "OFI Invest Asset Management Buys 8,671 Shares of Deckers Outdoor" — just share purchase data

   **Telltale signs of template stock articles** (almost always `mention_only`):
   - Phrases like "Get Free Report", "according to its most recent 13F filing"
   - "The stock has a 200-day moving average of $X" / "50-day moving average"
   - "Several other research analysts also recently issued reports"
   - "shares of the footwear maker" / "shares of the textile maker" / "shares of the apparel retailer"
   - Sources like MarketBeat, MarketScreener, HoldingsChannel, tickerreport.com, themarketsdaily.com

   **Template pages with an appended news summary** → `mention_only`
   The MarketBeat family's 13F holdings, options-volume and earnings-guidance templates often end with a section of recent news about the company (key stories, recent headlines). The summary repeats other outlets' reporting; it is not the page's own. The page is `mention_only` even when the summary reports ESG news or business results.

   **Pure stock price/volume articles** → `mention_only`
   - "NKE stock up 4% today" — just price movement
   - "Adidas (OTCMKTS:ADDYY) Sees Strong Trading Volume" — just volume data
   - "Allbirds Stock Price Up 1.5% – What Next?" — price + boilerplate company profile
   - "PUMA SE trading above 50-day moving average" — technical analysis only
   - "Anta Sports Products Sees Unusually-High Trading Volume" — just volume spike data

   **Short interest articles** → `mention_only`
   - "Short Interest in Adidas AG Rises By 88.3%" — just short interest numbers
   - "Deckers Outdoor Sees Significant Decline in Short Interest" — just share counts

   **Boilerplate analyst rating roundups** → `mention_only`
   These list analyst ratings/price targets WITHOUT explaining the reasoning behind them. They are just aggregated data:
   - "Columbia Sportswear Receives Consensus Rating of 'Hold' from Analysts" — just a list of ratings
   - "lululemon athletica Receives $227.15 Consensus PT from Analysts" — just averaged price targets
   - "Columbia Sportswear Lowered to Sell Rating by Wall Street Zen" — rating change + boilerplate, no reasoning
   - "Analysts Set Columbia Sportswear PT at $60.50" — just price target numbers
   - "lululemon athletica 'Market Perform' Rating Reiterated at Telsey Advisory" — rating + no analysis

   **Boilerplate stock comparison articles** → `mention_only`
   Side-by-side financial metric comparisons with no editorial analysis:
   - "Reviewing Victoria's Secret & Allbirds" — just P/E, revenue, margins tables
   - "Oxford Industries vs. Under Armour Head to Head Comparison" — just financial ratios
   - "Comparing Hennes & Mauritz and 361 Degrees International" — just metric comparisons

   **Auto-generated stock return calculators** → `mention_only`
   - "$100 Invested In Deckers Outdoor 20 Years Ago Would Be Worth This Much Today" — just math

   **Automated valuation templates** → `mention_only`
   Fair-value, intrinsic-value or discounted cash flow (DCF) articles that run the company's numbers through a standard model: projected cash flows, a discount rate, a terminal value, and an estimate set against the share price, often followed by a stock checklist ("strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats"). Sources like Simply Wall St. The figures are the company's, but nobody analyzed its business.

2. **Tangential mentions without substantive coverage:**
   - Former executives now at other companies (article is about the OTHER company)
   - Brand mentioned only as biographical context or comparison
   - Industry reports where brand appears in a list without detail
   - A competitor named as context ("Nike's biggest Chinese challenger")
   - **A market wrap or news roundup that gives the brand one or two lines** (futures and pre-market reports, "stocks moving today", daily briefings), even when that line gives a reason ("shares fell after a downgrade", "after weak quarterly profits"). The article's subject is the market or the day's news, not the brand.
   - **A brand named only as the goods or the store in an incident it is not a party to** (a theft of its products from a franchise or retailer's store), when the article reports nothing the company did or decided
   - **A brand named in a list of companies an event affects** ("retailers including Walmart, Target and Adidas face delays from the port strike"), when the article says nothing specific about that brand.

3. **Brand mentioned only as comparison, analogy, or reference point:**
   - Color comparisons: "reminiscent of Nike's Infrared orange", "similar to Adidas' classic blue"
   - Style comparisons: "gives off Patagonia vibes", "looks like something from Lululemon"
   - Quality/reputation references: "built to Nike standards", "Adidas-level durability"
   - Historical references: "back when Nike was just starting out"
   - Aspiration analogies: "wants to be the Patagonia of baby clothes"
   - The article's SUBJECT is something else; the brand is merely a point of reference

**`retail_only` and `product`: product articles** (sale listings, deals roundups, price-drop alerts, gift guides, reviews, releases). Decided by the Product-article rule below.

**Product-article rule** (the one rule for every product article; such articles never receive ESG categories, see "Product Reviews, Roundups, and Shopping Articles" above). Judge each brand by what the article gives THAT brand, not by the article's genre. Would it be useful under a future "Product" category?
- **`product`** — for this brand, the article describes or evaluates a specific product, or reports its release: what is new about it, how it is built, how it performs, how buyers say it holds up, or that it is releasing or launching and when. A release date counts on its own, however it is phrased: "releases November 14", "available from November 14" or "goes on sale November 14" for a product not sold before.
- **`retail_only`** — for this brand, the article gives only retail details: product names, prices, discounts, links, stock or where to buy, promo codes, a one-line sales blurb ("perfect for cold months"), or a line of specs (capacity, sizes, a feature list). A restock, or the dates a discount or sale event runs ("20% off until Sunday"), is a retail detail, not a release or a description.
- The genre is a hint, not the test. Reviews, release announcements and collaboration launches are usually `product`; sale listings, price-drop alerts, deals roundups and gift guides are usually `retail_only`. Decide by the content:
  - A release roundup ("A Ma Maniére Serves an Air Jordan 4 'Dark Mocha' in This Week's Best Footwear Drops") is `product` for each brand whose release it describes, and `retail_only` for a brand it only names with a price or a link.
  - A buying guide that tested the products ("Best running shoes of 2025: we ran 200 miles in each") is `product` for each brand whose product it assesses; a gift guide that lists products with prices ("Holiday gift guide: Best fitness gear") is `retail_only` for a brand it gives only a name, price and link.
  - A review ("Patagonia Nano Puff Hoody review", "I spent 6 months testing the Merrell Moab Speed 2s") is `product`, including one that also mentions a discount.
  - A sale listing that gives only the name, the price and a one-line blurb or a line of specs ("Under Armour Tech 2.0 tee now $15, 'a gym-bag staple'") or a sale section that lists products and prices ("lululemon 'We Made Too Much' Section February 2026") is `retail_only`.
  - A sale listing that also describes the product is `product`, whatever the sale framing: what it is made of, how it is built, how it performs, or how buyers say it holds up ("Brooks Running shoes: Adrenaline GTS 24 now $20 off", which describes its cushioning, support system and upper). One sentence is enough when it says how the product is built and what that does ("a carbon plate and a rocker sole for faster turnover"); a bare list of specs, or a blurb that only praises it ("a gym-bag staple"), is not.
  - A collaboration launch that describes the product ("Madhappy x Converse Chuck 70 collab") is `product`.
  - A release announcement ("New Balance 550 releases in new colorway") is `product`, including one that gives only the product, the date and a price ("Reebok Angel Reese 1 releases November 14").
- Check your reasoning against the type: if your reasoning says the article describes the product's materials, build, fit or performance, the type is `product`, not `retail_only`.

**`financial`: the article's subject is the company's stock, earnings, or an analyst's or investor's view of it, with human-written analysis or commentary.** Never receives ESG categories (see "Governance is NOT Financial News" above). The brand must be the subject, or one of a few companies the article analyzes in their own paragraphs: one or two lines in a market wrap, or a place in a list of companies, is `mention_only` however financial the line is.
- "Jim Cramer says Nike CEO is reinventing the company" — discusses strategy/management as an investment case
- "Analysts give Adidas Buy rating citing strong Q4 outlook" — business prospects with reasoning
- "Puma reports earnings beat driven by China growth" — business performance with context
- "Sinking 48%, Is Lululemon a Buying Opportunity?" — an investment thesis with business analysis
- "Nike Road to Recovery: Innovation, Competition, and Steady Dividend Growth" — strategy as an investment case
- "Needham downgrades Nike citing brand weakness in North America" — an analyst gives specific reasoning
- "Deckers Racks Up Record Revenue as Significant Global Demand for Ugg and Hoka Continues" — earnings with CEO quotes and segment analysis
- An earnings preview, a stock drop explained by tariffs or demand, or a refund or charge's effect on results is `financial`.

**Key test for financial articles:** Does the article contain **human-written analysis or commentary** about the brand's business (analyst reasoning, CEO/CFO quotes, strategy discussion, competitive analysis, what drove results)? Then `financial`. Is it **auto-generated/template content** that just plugs financial data into a standard format (raw prices, 13F share counts, moving averages, rating lists without reasoning, short interest, metric tables, "X invested Y years ago", a DCF or fair-value model)? Then `mention_only`. Headlines or story summaries appended under template data are not human-written analysis: the page is still `mention_only`. If the article reports a governance event (a board fight, a proxy battle, an activist stake or one that concentrates ownership, fraud, an ESG report, executive pay policy) or job cuts, rather than the stock's or results' outlook, it is `business`, even with stock figures in it.

**`business`: substantive news about the company itself** — strategy, operations, leadership, legal matters, store openings, sponsorships, athlete signings, partnerships, and ESG initiatives, policies, programs, commitments or controversies. This is the only content type whose ESG categories are kept, and the decision trees above still decide whether any category applies. A `business` brand with no ESG content (a store opening, a sponsorship) gets `applies: false` for every category.

**For every content type other than `business`, set `applies: false` for every category.** Categories given under any other content type are discarded.
